# The island in words

Everything that is on the island and in its sea, and where to look for it. The short version
is the [README](../README.md); the same things with a picture each are in the
[manual](../manual/Tropical%20Island%20-%20Manual.pdf), a field guide of 28 pages.

The island itself is imagined: no chart of the Seychelles shows it. Its plants and animals are
real, gathered from across the islands; no single island of the Seychelles holds all of them.

Bushes are not solid: you push through them. In deep water you swim, snorkel and dive. If you ever get wedged between rocks or trunks, you are put
back on the last firm ground you stood on (or press R).

## What's on the island

- **Landscape**: the start beach from the original Blender scene, on an island about 190 m wide
  and 420 m long: an east coast with a granite headland below the hill and a bay on either side
  of it, tapering south and north ends, rolling forested hills and a 44 m summit lookout reached
  by an open grassy ridge from an opening in the bushes just north of the start beach. Bare
  granite slabs, jointed and streaked dark by the rain, crop out where the hill is steep, rounded
  backs of rock come out of the forest floor, and the east headland is a granite platform running
  into the sea. A small stream starts at a seep below the summit's granite, runs down a gully
  over ledges and through rock pools to the south-west, and ends in a pond behind the beach
  (walk south along the start beach for about 150 m, then inland). No paths or buildings: the
  island looks untouched. A lagoon with seagrass meadows and coral heads surrounds it, out to a
  reef edge whose slope falls away into deep water; off the west beach the swell breaks on the
  reef crest in a white line. Distant islands and an anchored boat lie offshore.
- **Plants**: coconut palms on every shore, beach naupaka, Pemphis and other shrubs; a forest of
  takamaka (short thick trunks, heavy spreading limbs, dark glossy crowns) and badamier (Indian
  almond: branches in flat tiers like a pagoda, a few leaves turned red) with jackfruit trees
  (fruit growing straight from the trunk); screwpines (vacoa) on stilt roots among the granite
  boulders and along the beaches, with orange fruit heads; the Seychelles' own palms: latanier
  feuille, palmiste and the coco de mer with its giant fan leaves. The female coco de mer carry
  their huge green two-lobed fruit on hanging stalks, the males arm-long brown catkins; under the
  females lie fallen nuts, the bare dark double nut that the palm is famous for; around them stand
  young palms that have no trunk yet. A grove of Pisonia on the southern headland (massive pale
  trunks, heavy limbs, big soft leaves: the tree the seabirds nest in), ferns and bird's-nest ferns, and red
  mangroves on stilt roots where the forest meets the sea. Shells, coral pieces and coconuts lie
  along the strand lines. On the bare granite slabs just below the summit (a few steps south
  and west of the top) grow the plants of the Seychelles' rock outcrops: mats of the pitcher
  plant Nepenthes pervillei, found nowhere else, its goblets yellow, orange and red; the
  tropicbird orchid, the national flower, with its rows of white flowers; and the leafless
  wild vanilla, green ropes running over the rock with a few large white flowers.
- **Grotto**: a cave of the kind these granite islands have: not a hollow in the rock but the
  space under a pile of huge boulders leaning on one another, at the foot of the hill about 55 m
  inland from the start beach (head up the opening toward the ridge, then bear right). You go in
  through a cleft facing the sea, cross a low front room, follow a passage up into a high inner
  room where the roots of a tree that stands on the boulders above hang down through a gap in
  the roof (when the sun stands in line with the gap a shaft of sunlight falls through it onto the sand: by the clock between about nine and eleven in the morning from late October to late February, the months when the sun passes south of overhead; with T's midday at any time), and climb out through a second mouth higher up the
  hill, from where it is a short way to the ridge. Water drips and your footsteps echo.
- **Animals**: a pair of Aldabra giant tortoises grazing on the ridge bite by bite (they pull in
  head and legs if you come too close), skinks on rocks and the upper beach, green day geckos on palm trunks, ghost
  crabs that bolt for their burrows, crested terns that hover and plunge-dive for fish along the
  beach, a seabird colony on the wooded southern headland (noddies on nests, white terns,
  frigatebirds soaring overhead, and males displaying their red throat sacs in the treetops), reef fish around you when you wade, spotted eagle rays, young blacktip reef sharks a meter
  long patrolling the shallows with their fins out, now and then a tiger shark's fin beyond the
  surf, dark rock crabs on the boulders at the waterline, palm spiders in their golden webs
  above head height between two trunks, termite nests on palm trunks with the tiny workers
  running along their runways (look from a step away), a few mudskippers on the wet sand where
  the mangroves stand thickest (they skip into the water when you come near), golden dragonflies over
  the pond, the ridge and the summit, orange butterflies along the forest's edge, and flying
  foxes: by day hanging in a tree below the ridge, at night in the air. In the lagoon off the
  start beach live two sea turtles: a green turtle that grazes the seagrass and a smaller
  hawksbill among the corals; every minute or two each comes up for air, and you may see a head
  break the surface from the beach. And one manta ray, five meters across, cruises
  the deeper water of the same lagoon on slow wing beats, now and then just under the surface
  with its wing tips out. It takes no notice of you: swim out and it may pass a few meters away.
  The smaller animals keep their distance from you without panic: they move off, they do not bolt.
  White-tailed tropicbirds, white with black wing bars and two long tail streamers, fly about
  the granite of the summit and the east headland, pairs courting in the air; some sit on
  their nests in nooks at the foot of the boulders there. On the shore near you a small flock
  of ruddy turnstones works along the waterline, and three black-and-white crab plovers stand
  on the wet sand a little further off: the turnstones let you come within a few steps before
  they walk on ahead or fly a short way, the plovers keep a dozen meters between you and them.
  A striated heron stands hunched at the edge of the pond, stalking and striking; come close
  and it flies across the water.
  Now and then, by day (as only the Seychelles' hawksbills do), a hawksbill turtle comes ashore
  to nest on a beach near you: she hauls herself up the sand leaving a track like a small
  tractor's, digs, lays, covers the nest with flying sand and goes back to the sea by another
  way; the whole visit takes about five minutes. Keep three meters away while she crawls up
  and starts to dig, or she turns back without laying; once she lays nothing disturbs her.
  This morning's track and nest are on the start beach, some way north of where you begin.
  And occasionally an old nest hatches near you: forty-odd hatchlings scramble down to the sea.
  By the stream's rock pools and the pond lives the rare Seychelles frog, found on only a few of
  these islands: thumbnail-sized, brown and warty, sitting on the damp ground or a rock by the
  water. There are seven on the whole island; look closely, and listen for a thin high trill.
  One giant grouper, the biggest fish of the reef (1.7 m here; they grow far bigger), lives
  over a patch of open sand at the foot of the reef some 65 m out from the start beach, five
  meters down. It hangs there sculling with its fins, watches you come, and moves aside
  without haste when you get within a couple of meters.
  Two triggerfish have territories on the reef off the start beach: a titan triggerfish, the
  biggest of its family (0.7 m, yellow-olive with a net of dark scales and black-edged fins),
  in the shallower reef some 30 m out to the south-west, and a clown triggerfish (black with
  big white spots and orange lips) further out to the north-west where it is deeper. They
  swim with the two fins on their back and belly, the body stiff, and tip head down to pick at
  the floor; they keep a couple of meters from you.
  A few Moorish idols live on the reef in twos and threes, each little group at a coral
  head of its own: white, black and yellow, the back fin drawn out into a long white
  streamer. They travel in a loose file from coral to coral, tip head down to pick at the
  reef, and move off together when you come within a couple of meters. The nearest pair
  is some 35 m out from the start beach, a little to the south; three live in the coral
  garden, two more to the north. The schools that come to you when you wade are blue
  tangs, convict surgeonfish (pale, with thin black bars), sergeants, parrotfish and
  chromis.
  Two great barracudas hang at the reef's edge, some 90 m straight out from the start beach
  where the floor falls away (about six meters of water: as far out as you can swim). Long
  silver fish, a meter and more, they hang almost still a couple of meters under the surface,
  one a little behind and below the other, and move on along the edge every minute or so.
  They let you come to four or five meters and then slide off to seaward, without haste.
  In the seabird colony on the southern headland birds also nest on the ground, as they do
  on islands that have never had rats or cats: white-tailed tropicbirds lie in the nooks at
  the feet of the trees, their long tail feathers on the leaf litter (here and there a chick
  sits alone instead: a ball of pale down, its parents at sea), and brown noddies sit on
  little nests of twigs on the open ground. None of them leaves when you walk up: they turn
  their heads and stay.
  And on the open sand at the top of the beach just west of the colony's grove sits a colony
  of sooty terns, the ground breeder that fills whole islands of the Seychelles: some seventy
  black-and-white birds a step apart, most on their single egg, all facing into the wind, a
  few wheeling overhead; an egg lies on the bare sand at the feet of each bird that has stood
  up, and here and there a chick, a small ball of buff down, crouches beside its parent. You
  hear the colony long before you see it, and among the birds it is loud. You can walk up to
  them; they watch you and stay.
  At half tide and low water the tide pools on the granite of the east headland are small
  seas of their own: rockskippers lie on the floor and dart to the far side when your shadow
  falls on the pool, long-spined urchins sit in the deepest corners, a cushion star in some,
  and nerite snails on the rock around the rim. The granite between the tide marks, on the
  headland and on every boulder the sea reaches, carries barnacles: ribbed volcano barnacles
  in the middle of the tidal range, small pale star barnacles above them up to the
  high-water mark, in crowds with bare rock between. Low tide shows the whole belt.
  Four small birds of the land keep you company. A pair of common mynahs struts about open
  ground, brown with black heads and yellow bills, wary; three barred ground doves patter
  about the top of the beach picking at the sand and let you come close (both were brought
  to the Seychelles long ago and are everywhere there now). On the forest floor three
  Seychelles fodies hop about almost underfoot, and now and then, rarely, a Seychelles
  magpie-robin turns up: glossy black with a white shoulder, one of the rarest birds there
  is. Come too near and a group flies a little way off; none of them at night or in rain.
  Each has its voice: the mynahs' loud chatter above all, the dove's soft rolling coo.
  Now and then a ripe coconut drops from a palm somewhere near you (never close) and lands
  with a thud. The nesting turtle breathes heavily as she works, and the sand she throws
  patters down; a turtle in the lagoon blows when its head comes up.
  And now and then, by day, a pod of spinner dolphins passes just outside the reef: dark
  backs rolling over the surface, and now and then one that leaps clear and spins before it
  falls back with a splash. From the start beach they are about a hundred meters off; swim
  out to the reef's edge and they pass at fifteen to thirty meters. They come every eight to
  fourteen minutes, while you are on the sea side of the island or high on the hill.
  Land hermit crabs walk the beaches in borrowed snail shells: a few by day in the shade at the
  top of the beach, many from the golden hour through the night and whenever rain has wetted
  the ground. Come within a step and one snaps back into its shell; stand still and after a
  few seconds it looks out and walks on. At the same hours, and after rain, the forest floor
  has its own walkers: the Seychelles giant millipede, glossy black, a hand long, its legs
  moving in waves; and, rarely, the big brown Seychelles snail. Look at the ground ahead of you,
  and at the trunks: at those hours some of the millipedes climb the palms and trees.
- **Diving**: while swimming, press Space: a duck dive, and you swim where you look, on one
  breath, down to four and a half meters or the sea floor: level with the garden eels, the
  octopus in its den, the grouper. You glide up and over the corals, not through them. After
  45 seconds (the edges of the view grow dim for the last ten) you rise calmly by yourself;
  Space again, or simply swimming up, ends a dive sooner. At the surface you take a deep
  breath, and need ten seconds before the next dive.
- **Snorkeling**: walk into the sea until you float, then look down: your face goes under
  water. Seagrass meadows sway on the sand, and the coral heads and the reef are crowded with
  corals: staghorn thickets, cauliflower and finger corals, boulder and brain corals, tables,
  whorls of plates, fire coral, leather corals and sea fans, with black long-spined sea urchins
  clustered at their feet (they only look dangerous: nothing here hurts), blue sea stars and
  cushion stars, and giant clams set in the rock with their bright mantles showing. Red-knobbed
  sea stars lie on the sand and in the seagrass. The reef fish, rays, turtles and young sharks
  move among it all. You can swim anywhere in the lagoon, out to the
  reef edge.
  Look for the reef's characters, each at home in one place: Seychelles anemonefish (found
  only on these islands) in the carpet anemones, a pair and their young in each, and skunk
  anemonefish in the long-tentacled magnificent anemones on top of the coral heads; garden
  eels standing out of the bare sand like a field of question marks, which sink into their
  burrows as you come near and rise again behind you; a honeycomb or a giant moray looking out
  of a hole in a lump of reef rock, mouth opening and closing as it breathes (that is all it
  does: it draws back if you hang right over it); the day octopus in its den, shells and
  stones before the door, which now and then climbs out to sit on top of its rock and turns
  dark or pale as you watch; and a few lionfish hanging by a big coral with their fans
  spread. With your face in the water you hear the parrotfish crunching coral.
  The light plays under water as it does in the sea: a moving net of bright lines over the
  sand, the corals and the animals, shafts of sunlight slanting down through the water (best
  in the afternoon, looking toward the sun), a swirl of bubbles when your face goes under
  and with every stroke, and in bright sun strings of tiny bubbles rising from the seagrass.
  Moon jellyfish drift in the lagoon a meter or two down, a few together, clear bells with
  four pale rings that beat slowly; the first group is on the way from the start beach out
  to the coral garden. They take no notice of you and do not sting.
  With your head under water you hear the reef: the fine crackle of snapping shrimp (as
  loud as there is coral about: hardly there over sand and seagrass, at its fullest in the
  coral garden), the crunch of a parrotfish, and on a dive the bubbles going up. The coral
  garden lies straight out from the start beach, some 75 m, on the crest the swell breaks
  on and in the pass through it: a belt of reef covered with coral from end to end.
- **Sea**: swell, breaking waves, swash running up the sand, foam, clear water that shades from
  turquoise to deep blue. On some nights the sea glows: a bloom of Noctiluca lights the surf,
  your wake and your wet footprints blue. It comes about one night in three, by the date (some
  glowing nights follow each other, and so do dark ones); B calls it for tonight, or sends it away.
- **Time of day**: the island's day is yours: it follows your computer's clock, with sunrise
  at about 6:20 in the east (behind the island) and sunset at about 18:20 in the west (over
  the sea off the start beach), as near the equator all the year. The sun goes the way it
  really goes over the Seychelles (4.6 degrees south of the equator) on today's date: from
  November to January it passes south of overhead and sets to the left of straight out from
  the start beach, from May to July it passes to the north and sets to the right; at the
  equinoxes it rises due east, stands almost overhead at noon and sets due west. In the
  evening you arrive at night: T gives any hour you like (midday, afternoon, golden hour,
  sunset, night) and, after the last, the clock again. T's afternoon, golden hour and
  sunset show the sun where it stands on today's path; T's midday is a fixed look, a
  mid-morning sun of December, and the one that sends the shaft of light into the grotto.
- **Weather**: rarely, a rain shower passes, at its natural pace. The sky closes and the wind
  gets up, then it rains for a quarter of an hour or a little more, swelling and easing:
  rain falling around you, rings on the sea and the pools, the sand and the rocks dark and
  wet, the distance gray, lizards and butterflies gone. Toward the end the sun comes back
  while the last light rain falls, and if it stands low enough there is a rainbow far off
  on the sky opposite the sun, faint, often double: high at golden hour and sunset (when it
  is red and dim), low in the afternoon (best from the summit or the east coast), none at
  midday. While it rains the wind is about twice as strong and the trees show it. Under
  overhanging granite it stays dry. In the
  grotto the same rain comes down through the gaps in the roof, thinly, and what runs off
  the rock drips from their rims and from the tips of the roots that hang through, for
  some minutes after the shower too. Afterward the ground dries, the
  trees drip and the frogs call. A shower never comes in the first twenty minutes and then
  only by chance, about once in a hundred minutes; N calls one, and pressed again while it
  rains lets it pass.
  While it rains the drops splash on the ground, and when the sun comes back the wet leaves
  and palm fronds glint until everything has dried.
- **Tide**: the sea rises and falls by the real clock, at its real pace: high water every
  12 hours and 25 minutes, with a bigger range at spring tides and a smaller one at neaps.
  So the shore you find depends on the hour you start, and if you stay for hours it moves.
  At high water the sea stands at the mangroves' roots; at low water the beaches are some
  five meters wider, the crest of the reef comes out with the surf breaking around it, and
  on the granite of the east headland tide pools are left. The shorebirds, the mudskippers
  and the young sharks follow the water's edge. G sets the tide by hand: high water, half
  tide, low water, and back to the clock.
- **Moon**: its phase is the real one for today's date, and it crosses the sky as the real
  moon does: a full moon rises behind the island as the sun sets, stands high at midnight
  (to the north of overhead when the sun is in the south, and the other way around)
  and goes down over the sea before dawn; a young crescent hangs over the sea after sunset,
  lying on its back like a boat; at new moon the night belongs to the stars, which turn
  with the hours. The moon's light follows its phase and its height. The tide follows the
  moon: spring tides at new and full moon, high water about 50 minutes later every day.
  M sets a phase by hand.
- **Sky**: drifting clouds colored by the sun; at night a southern starfield, the Milky Way and a
  moon path on the water. (The stars are a painted sky that turns as the real one does. Planned
  for a later stage: real constellations, the stars by the season, and the finer points of the
  sun's and the moon's year.)
- **Sound**: waves along the nearest shore in step with the visible swash, wind that moves the
  foliage, rustling palms, forest birds, night insects and frogs, the seabird colony, the
  tortoise munching, and footsteps on dry sand, wet sand and in water.
